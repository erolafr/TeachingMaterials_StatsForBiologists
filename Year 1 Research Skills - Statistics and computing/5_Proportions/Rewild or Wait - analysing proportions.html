1. Context of the data

The rare alpine flower Forget-me-not (Eritrichium nanum) has disappeared two years ago from our regional mountain range due to warming and reduced snowpack. The seed bank holds 5,000 viable seeds collected before local extinction. You are the Alpine Restoration Committee and you need to make a decision: Should we attempt reintroduction this year?

Unfortunately, funds are limited and you can’t recommend reintroduction unless you are certain that it can be successful, not only due to the limited funding but also due to the limited amount of seeds (i.e. you have only one opportunity to reintroduce).

Specifically, the conservation fund will support a reintroduction only if estimated germination proportion ≥ 0.40. Additionally, the Board requires that we be reasonably certain that true germination is not below 0.30 (they asked for about 95% confidence that true p > 0.30). Seeds have been stored in optimal conditions for preservation, and eventhough some seed ageing might happen (i.e. seeds will be less likely to remain viable with storage time), scientific evidence from similar species reveal that specific treatments can contribute to break seed dormancy.

PAUSE: In case you are not familiar with artificial seed banks and seed dormancy, just a quick non-scientific language note: Seeds from some species can go dormant, meaning they will wait for germination until they receive certain environmental cues (increase in the number of light hours or increase of temperature, meaning spring is coming, as well as low temperatures for a certain period might indicate winter is over, or even “feeling” an acidic environment means seeds have gone through the digestive system of an animal and have been dispersed far enough from the mother, this is called scarification). In artificial seed banks, researchers explore the optimal conditions to preserve seeds in a dormant state (each species have evolved to respond to different environmental cues), as well as the optimal conditions to “wake all seeds up”, maintaining high viability. Plants have evolved to not always germinate 100% for different reasons, including a bed-hedging strategy. Natural germination rates could range from 20 to 80%. This would mean, that if 30% germination is expected, every time we want to get some plants from the seed bank, we will be wasting 70% of the preserved sample. For this reason, unraveling which conditions are required for optimal germination is essential in seed bank management.

Let’s get back to the case. For the alpine flower Forget-me-not, you have started small pilot trials to compare two “seed awakening” (dormancy break) treatments:

  • Cold treatment (current protocol). This method mimics natural alpine winter conditions. Seeds are stored at 4 degrees for 8 weeks. The cold period simulates snow cover and winter chilling. It is cheap and logistically simple, requiring only refrigeration space.

  • Warm treatment (experimental). This method simulates early spring soil warming. Seeds are first hydrated and then exposed to controlled warm temperatures (day: 15 degrees, night, 5 degrees) for 10 weeks. Some alpine seeds respond strongly to temperature fluctuations.

For each treatment you use 12 petri dishes (or trays), 25 seeds per petri dishes. Your job is to analyse the pilot data, quantify uncertainty, and recommend Proceed or Delay.

The rules:

  • Snowpack conditions are ideal this year

  • Funding is limited

  • You have one opportunity to reintroduce

Management rules (given):

  • Proceed with reintroduction if estimated germination proportion p_hat ≥ 0.40 and you are confident the true p is not below 0.30 (the Board wants ~95% confidence p > 0.30).

  • Delay. If results are borderline, discuss risk and recommend more trials or a pilot field test.

Risk Matrix (use this to decide):

  • Green (Proceed): lower bound of 95% CI > 0.40 → proceed.

  • Amber (Caution / Conditional Proceed): 95% CI includes 0.40 but lower bound > 0.30 → risky but consider limited pilot reintroductions or more replication.

  • Red (Delay): upper bound of 95% CI < 0.40 OR lower bound ≤ 0.30 → do not proceed; collect more data or improve protocol.

8. Simulation demo: what does a p-value really mean?

We tested if germination under the cold treatment was equal to 0.40. When doing this, the binomial test gave us a p-value. But what does that number actually represent? (Make sure you know where this number was in the questions above).

This p-value represents the following: If the true germination probability really were 0.40, how often would we observe results as extreme as ours just by random chance? Let’s simulate it!

We will assume that true germination probability is 0.40 and we planted n_cold seeds (300). If that is true, then the number of germinated seeds follows a binomial distribution. We will simulate many repeated experiments under that assumption.

# Number of simulated experiments
B <- 5000  

# Simulate B experiments under H0: p = 0.40
simulated_successes <- rbinom(B, size = n_cold, prob = 0.40)

# Look at first six simulated outcomes (numer of seeds germinated)
head(simulated_successes)
[1] 138 124 119 109 114 119

What this does is generate a random numbers dataset from a binomial distribution. Each number represents one hypothetical experiment (how many seeds germinated from the 300 we used). We repeat the experiment 5,000 times.

What we actually observed was:

x_cold
[1] 131

Now we ask: In our simulated world (where p = 0.40), how often do we see results as far away from expectation as our real data? The expected number of successes under H0 is:

expected_null <- n_cold * 0.40
expected_null
[1] 120

Now we compute how extreme our observation was:

observed_distance <- abs(x_cold - expected_null)
observed_distance
[1] 11

Now count how many simulations were at least that extreme:

p_simulated <- mean(abs(simulated_successes - expected_null) >= observed_distance)
p_simulated
[1] 0.223

This the proportion of simulated experiments that were as extreme as or more extreme than what we observed. That is the p-value, and it should be close to the p-value from:

  • If we increased n_cold (sample size), would the spread of simulated results get wider or narrower?

ANSWER:

Possible answer: The spread of proportions shrinks like sqrt(p(1-p)/n)​, so the proportion values become narrower (more precise).

  • With a larger sample size, would extreme deviations become more or less likely?

ANSWER:

Possible answer: Less likely. Larger n makes the sample proportion cluster tightly around the true p, so large proportional deviations from the null are rarer. (In absolute counts a big deviation may still appear but is judged relative to a larger SD.)

  • If the true proportion of germinated seeds were 0.38 instead of 0.40, would the p-value necessarily be small?

ANSWER:

Possible answer: No. A p-value depends on the observed data and the sample size. A true difference of 0.02 (0.38 vs 0.40) is small; with n=300 it’s unlikely to give a very small p unless the observed outcome happens to be far from 0.40. Larger samples can detect such small differences, small samples usually cannot. Global takeaway: bigger n means more precision for proportions; small effect sizes can be statistically detectable only with sufficiently large samples, so always interpret p-values together with effect size and sample size.

FINAL NOTE: Statistics does not tell us what to do. It tells us how uncertain we are when we decide.

Possible answer: Changing the probabilities, number of replicates, or seeds per Petri dish clearly shows how strongly sample size influences our conclusions. When we increase the number of seeds or replicates, confidence intervals become narrower and p-values more stable, meaning we can estimate germination rates with greater precision. With only 10 seeds per dish, random variation is much larger, one or two seeds can change the proportion dramatically, making it harder to detect real differences between treatments. In contrast, 100 seeds per dish greatly reduces uncertainty, because each individual seed has less influence on the final proportion. However, this decision clearly impacts the lab work and cost of the experiment. Whether 10 seeds are “enough” depends on the expected germination proportion and the size of the effect we want to detect. If germination is very high (e.g. 80–90%) or very low (e.g. 5–10%), even small samples might show clear differences. However, when proportions are around 40–60%, as in our case, variability is highest, so larger sample sizes are needed to confidently detect moderate differences (e.g. 10%). Therefore, experimental design should always consider the expected proportion and the minimum biologically meaningful difference before deciding how many seeds or replicates to use. The number of seeds per dish and the number of replicates directly control statistical precision. Good experimental design is not about using as many seeds as possible — it is about using enough to detect the effect size that matters biologically.
